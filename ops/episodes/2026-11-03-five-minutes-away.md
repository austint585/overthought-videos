# Five minutes away

- **id:** 2026-11-03-five-minutes-away
- **Source idea:** ours. The friend who is always "five minutes away", explained with two real findings: the planning fallacy and a study where laid-back people felt a minute pass at 77 seconds. Written so a viewer sends it to one specific friend; the last line ("Send him this. He'll see it in five minutes.") is the share prompt and loops to the first.

Numbers live on dark signs in the scene that count up. Follows the rotation: the episode before it is the toon cuffing season forecast, and before that the clay Bowerbird.

## Facts and sources

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| "Planning fallacy" is the psychologists' name for underestimating how long your own tasks take (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) | https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy |
| Students asked how long their honors thesis would take guessed 33.9 days on average; it took 55.5 days (rounded to 34 and 56 in the script) | Buehler, Griffin and Ross, "Exploring the planning fallacy", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 67(3):366-381 (1994): https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf ; summary: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planning_fallacy |
| Their average worst-case guess (48.6 days) was still about a week short | Same paper; Wikipedia summary above |
| Asked to judge when a minute had passed without a clock, Type B participants said it at about 77 seconds (Type A at about 58) | Jeff Conte, San Diego State University (2001 study, reported in Psychology Today, Pacific Standard and others): https://psmag.com/social-justice/theyre-just-super-polite-lol/ ; https://www.today.com/today/amp/tdna101718 ; Conte's abstract page: https://jeffconte.sdsu.edu/abstract1.html |

## Assumptions and jokes (not claims)

- "He believes it" and the shower reveal: the joke. The planning fallacy is about honest underestimates, which is why "not lying" fits.
- "Some people's minutes are just longer": framing of the time-estimation finding; the study shows a difference in perceived time, not that it causes lateness. On-screen note names the study.
- "His five minutes is about six and a half of yours": our math, 5 x 77/60 = 6.4, labeled "Our math" on screen. It assumes the friend is Type B.
- "Plan for twenty": a joke, labeled "A joke, not a study" on screen.
- Calendar page numbers are decoration.
