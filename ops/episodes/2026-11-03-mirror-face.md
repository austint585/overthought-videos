# She is not unphotogenic

- **id:** 2026-11-03-mirror-face
- **Source idea:** ours. A relatable looks joke people send to one friend: the friend who deletes every photo of herself and says "that's not what I look like". Two real studies explain it: people prefer their mirror image because it is the face they see most, and close-up selfies widen the nose.

Cast: a toy wig-stand head with a hair swoop and a mole on one side, so the mirror flip is visible. ## Facts and sources

| Claim in the script | Source |
|---|---|
| In 1977, people were shown a true print and a mirror-reversed print of their own face; they preferred the mirror print, their friends preferred the true print; explained by the mere exposure effect | Mita, Dermer and Knight, "Reversed facial images and the mere-exposure hypothesis", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 35(8):597-601 (1977). Summaries: Psychology Today https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-clarity/202209/why-your-photos-dont-look-like-you ; Frontiers in Psychology https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2010.00039/pdf |
| At 12 inches a selfie makes the nose look about 30% wider (nasal base 30% for men, 29% for women) | Ward, Ward, Fried and Paskhover, "Nasal distortion in short-distance photographs: the selfie effect", JAMA Facial Plastic Surgery (2018), doi:10.1001/jamafacial.2018.0009. Coverage: https://consumer.healthday.com/cosmetic-surgery-29/nose-surgery-500/nasal-distortive-effect-described-in-selfies-731642.html ; https://zmescience.com/science/news-science/selfies-make-hate-nose-02032018 |
| At 5 feet (1.5 m, standard portrait distance) there is no difference | Same study and coverage. |

## Assumptions and jokes (not claims)

- "She is not unphotogenic", "it's what she looks like to you": the joke frame.
- The 30% is from a computer model of an average face, not measured on real people; the on-screen note says "a modeled face".
- The polaroid counter ("0 KEPT THIS WEEK") is a gag, not data.
- The swelling nose in the selfie shot shows the effect, exaggerated in depth for readability; the width change is about 30%.
- Notes on screen name both studies; the last shot carries "Data: Mita et al. 1977, Ward et al. 2018".
